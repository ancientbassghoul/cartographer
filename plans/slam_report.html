Performance investigation · monocular drone mapping

Something accumulates in flight

Four sessions of instrumenting a MASt3R-SLAM pipeline on a single laptop GPU. Two architectural changes measured honestly — one rejected, one shipped — and then the finding that reframes both: the pipeline is not slow because of its algorithms. It is slow because something degrades while flying, and the same frames replayed offline do not degrade at all.

Stack
MASt3R-SLAM → voxel map → autopilot, 5 processes on ZMQ
Hardware
RTX 3080 Laptop, 16 GB · shared with the Unity sim
Input
Single monocular feed · no depth, IMU or pose

12 instrumented flights + 14 frame-exact replays
01

Verdict

Solved — session 68

It degrades only in flight

tracker_ms is flat at ~250–400 ms across a deterministic replay of the exact frames a flight consumed — and 2 000–6 000 ms on the live flight those frames came from. Same code, same frames, same order.

The cause is not in this codebase. The GPU thermally throttles to 210 MHz of a 2 100 MHz ceiling within two minutes of sustained load. See section 03.

Shipped

Bounding the graph works

The append-only factor graph now solves a suffix window. 42–55 % cheaper above 30 keyframes, identical below it. Real, measured under an ABBA design — and not the fix for the above.

Negative result

Threading the backend does not help

Works by every internal measure, makes real flights worse. One GPU, so the tracker competes with it. Shipped disabled.

02

The measurement that changed the question

For three sessions every conclusion came from live flights, and live flights are not repeatable — different trajectory, different tracking luck, different keyframe count. The fourth session made replay frame-exact, and that is what exposed the real problem.

The enabling discovery is unglamorous. SLAM runs at ~0.2 Hz, so the capture process hands it the newest frame and drops the backlog. Measured on one flight:

Live SLAM never sees video. It sees a slideshow ~1.9 s apart, with wildly uneven gaps. Every offline replay this project had ever run used a fixed stride, feeding frames 17 ms apart — near-identical images, so the match fraction never fell below threshold and keyframes barely formed: 3 keyframes in 200 frames offline against 52 in 395 live. The “bench it offline first” advice was measuring a regime the drone never flies in.

The fix was to log which frames SLAM actually consumed (rec_frame, already present in the message bus but never written to disk) and replay exactly those. That turns a flight into a controlled experiment: identical input, identical order, any config.

03

The accumulator

With frame-exact replay available, the same 319 frames were run offline and compared against the live flight they were recorded from. tracker_ms is tracker.track() — a Gauss-Newton pose solve, up to max_iters: 50, warm-started from the previous frame.

Every live flight starts at ~370 ms — the drone is still on the ground — and then steps up by 7–17× within two minutes of takeoff and never returns. The replays, fed the identical frames, stay flat.

What this rules out

It is not the frames. The replay consumes exactly the images the live flight consumed, in exactly the same order.

It is not any of the optimisation work. The step-change is identical with the bounded window ON and OFF, and with the backend inline or threaded.

It is not simply GPU contention with the simulator. That is real — replays run 1.0–1.4 s/frame against 2.4–7.3 s/frame live — but it is a constant across every flight, and cannot explain a step change at takeoff or why two flights with the same config differ 2×.

Something accumulates or changes state once the vehicle is airborne, inside a process whose inputs are provably identical. That is the whole problem. Everything below is smaller.

What the instrumentation said Flown Hypothesis rejected

Session 67 wrapped tracker.track() (not copied — third_party/ stays pristine) to log, per call: the Gauss-Newton iteration count, the convergence reason, the match / inlier count the solve started from, and a fixed-work / solve-work split (trk_pre_ms / trk_solve_ms). It flew on 2026-09-07, and it killed the hypothesis it was built to test.

Gauss-Newton iterations are flat. Median 4–7 for the entire flight, no trend. There is exactly one step — 1 → 6 at t = 22 s, when the drone first moves, because a stationary camera converges in a single step. The warm-start idx_f2k feedback loop is dead as an explanation.

What grew instead grew everywhere, by the same factor. trk_pre_ms 341 → 3 024 ms; solver cost normalised per iteration per 100 k correspondences 13.1 → 138.7 (10.6×). A ViT-Large forward pass and a 7-DOF Gauss-Newton step share no code, no kernels and no data, yet they move in lockstep (r = +0.878) and recover together. CPU work (frame_ms) stayed 4.8 → 6–9 ms throughout.

The one weak claim, tested rather than asserted. An earlier draft called trk_pre_ms “fixed work”. It is not — iter_proj and refine_matches are genuinely data-dependent. Measured: within the first five minutes, worst-half match quality gave trk_pre_ms 344 ms and best-half 343 ms. That channel is ~0.

The answer: the GPU was running at one tenth of its clock

Nothing in the stack logged the GPU, so session 68 added gpu_probe.py — standalone, pure stdlib, no project imports and no GPU work of its own. Global state from nvidia-smi (clocks, temperature, power, and the throttle reason bits); per-process VRAM and 3D/Compute engine share from Windows performance counters, because nvidia-smi --query-compute-apps reports per-process memory as [N/A] under WDDM.

It flew behind a deliberately controlled manual profile — park 2 min, fly 4, park 2, fly 4 — because a leak cannot un-leak while parked, so recoveries discriminate causes that a monotone autonomous trend never could.

SwThermalSlowdown was active in 235 of 247 samples. Median clock for the flight: 210 MHz against a 2 100 MHz ceiling. Peak 96 °C.

So the headline of this report was wrong, and wrong in an instructive way. “Steps up 7–17× within two minutes of takeoff, on every flight” was never about takeoff. It is the GPU's thermal time constant — throttling engages at 54 s and bottoms out at 146 s whatever the drone is doing. The operator's own flight proves it: during the first parked phase, with the aircraft stationary, slam_ms still went 350 → 2 000 ms. Motion was never involved. Every ruled-out item in the panel above was ruled out correctly; the hypothesis they left standing was simply the wrong shape, because no measurement in this stack could see below the CUDA API.

Still unexplained ~1.9× of the ~7×

If the clock were the whole story, time × clock — the cycle count for fixed work — would be constant. It is not, quite:

257 M cycles at 600–1 000 MHz · 263 M at 300–600 MHz · 482 M below 300 MHz. So the clock accounts for ~3.7× and something nearly doubles the cycle count at the floor. Untested candidates: VRAM spill to system memory (rose +210 MB; peak 10.2 / 16.4 GB), the simulator's rising share, sub-300 MHz scheduling cliffs.

The simulator's share is real but confounded. Xlab_util_3d correlates +0.955 with trk_pre_ms — the strongest number in the dataset, and consistent with the operator's long-held observation that facing the east wall (windows, an opening into another room) is measurably slower. But an 8×-slower GPU raises everyone's busy fraction, so this data cannot separate “Unity demanded more” from “everything got slower”.

The next step Do this first Ten minutes

The GPU idles at 64–65 °C with nothing running (35–45 °C would be normal) and draws only ~50 W at 96 °C, on a part that sustains 80–150 W. The cooling system is not removing 50 watts. That is dust, thermal paste, a blocked intake, a soft surface or a fan curve — not a workload problem, and not a software problem.

Raise or actively cool the machine, then re-run only the two-minute park with gpu_probe.py and watch where SwThermalSlowdown engages and what the clock floor becomes. Nothing else needs to change to know whether it worked.

Everything measured before this was measured at ~10 % clock. The bounded window, the map-publish cache and the backend-threading negative result are all real and were all benched on a pathological baseline — they are worth re-measuring, not re-doing. The relationship is also self-reinforcing in our favour: less GPU work → less heat → higher clock.

05

Bounding the graph — built, measured, shipped

The factor graph is append-only: get_unique_kf_idx() returns every keyframe ever seen in any edge, and solve_GN_rays() re-optimises all of them on every keyframe. local_opt.window_size existed in config, was read once at global_opt.py:26, and was never referenced again — dead code.

Two findings made this safe to cut. The CUDA kernel already remaps a non-contiguous keyframe set via searchsorted, so dropping edges needs no index surgery. And num_fix = 1 pins the lowest-indexed keyframe in the solve — which, for a suffix window, is always an out-of-window anchor, so a windowed solve auto-anchors to the existing global estimate.

06

Two traps that cost real time

  • Measuring a device by asking it questions

    The first frame-exact A/B said the window made things 10.3 % worse. The tell: it cost ~1.8 s more per solve even below the window, where the cut is a provable no-op. The selection was asking CUDA four separate questions per solve — ii.max(), a boolean-indexed anchor list, its .numel(), and mask.all() — each a device→host sync draining the queue before the solve could start. The edge lists are ~1.3 KB; pulling them to the host once and deciding there fixed it: 2 517 → 749 ms, against the unwindowed 742.

  • Run order, on this machine, is worth ~26 %

    Identical code, identical input, different position in a sequential batch: 358.8 s vs 453.0 s. That is larger than the effect being measured, and it is what contaminated the first verdict. Every comparison here now uses an ABBA design so a linear drift cancels. Any future benchmark on this hardware that compares two sequential runs is measuring the order.

07

Known defects, carried forward

The machine thermally throttles to 10 % clock Root cause · not a code defect

Carried at the top of this list because it dominates every other entry. The GPU idles at 64–65 °C and reaches 96 °C at only ~50 W, on a part rated for 80–150 W; SwThermalSlowdown holds the core at 210 MHz of a 2 100 MHz ceiling for 95 % of a flight. See section 03.

Consequence for everything below: every constant-factor result in this report was measured on a GPU running at roughly a tenth of its clock. The findings stand as relative comparisons — they were ABBA-controlled — but their absolute magnitudes are not the magnitudes this stack would show on healthy hardware.

The bounded window leaks Confirmed on flight data

Loop-closure edges pull out-of-window keyframes back into the solve as anchors, and num_fix = 1 pins only the oldest. Measured at W=30: solve_kf reached 42 (30 + 12 anchors), anchors>1 on 25 of 102 solves, anchor drift to 0.489. Under the shipped write-back policy that drift is discarded — those keyframes are optimised and the answer thrown away. The bound overshoots by 43 %.

Fix: make num_fix a kernel parameter. Anchors are always the lowest sorted indices, so “fix the first k” is exactly “fix all anchors” — no reordering, no permutation buffer. Requires rebuilding the CUDA extension.

Input resolution Previously parked — on a false premise

This was parked as “a bench experiment, not a flight, since raw recordings exist”. Recordings existed but did not reproduce live conditions at any fixed stride, so that reasoning was void. With frame-exact replay it is now genuinely benchable — though it remains a constant-factor trade that pushes the model off its training distribution.

Two GPUs, or CUDA stream priority Speculative

The threading negative result is specific to one GPU, which is also rendering the simulator. The backend is genuinely parallelisable work with nowhere to go.

08

Where a second opinion would help most

  • What explains the ~1.9× that the clock does not?

    Cycle counts for fixed work should be flat across clock bands. They are — 257 M at 600–1 000 MHz, 263 M at 300–600 MHz — and then jump to 482 M below 300 MHz. Something nearly doubles the work per cycle at the floor. VRAM spill to system memory rose +210 MB over the flight (peak 10.2 / 16.4 GB); the simulator's engine share rose; sub-300 MHz may simply have its own scheduling cliff. Which of those is worth chasing first?

  • How much of the GPU is the simulator actually taking?

    Xlab_util_3d correlates +0.955 with tracker cost, and looking at one particular wall — windows, an opening into a second room — is reproducibly the slowest thing the drone can do. But utilisation percentages are busy-fractions, so an 8×-slower GPU inflates every process's share at once. What measurement separates “the renderer demanded more work” from “everything got slower” on a single shared GPU?

  • Is 0.2 Hz simply what this model costs on this hardware?

    Still open, and now askable honestly for the first time. If ~1 s per ViT-L pass at 512 px on a mobile 3080 sharing a GPU with the renderer is near the floor at full clock, the answer is architectural — a lighter tracker between keyframes — rather than optimisation. Every previous attempt to answer this was measuring a throttled machine.

  • What should we have measured first?

    Offered as the transferable lesson rather than a question. Five sessions of increasingly careful software instrumentation correctly ruled out the frames, the solver, the backend, the map and the warm start — and could not see the cause, because nothing in the stack could see below the CUDA API. A hardware counter would have cost ten minutes on day one.
